Professional Growth and Goals

Completing my Computer Science degree has helped me become more confident in learning technical concepts, working through problems, and turning ideas into working software. I have learned that development is not just about writing code that runs. It also means understanding the problem, choosing an appropriate approach, testing carefully, communicating decisions, and making changes without creating new problems. I learn best by building things and seeing how the pieces work together, so hands-on projects have been especially valuable to me.

My software engineering internship in the aerospace industry has allowed me to apply these skills in a professional setting. My work has included Python scripts, firmware testing, automated tests, log analysis, and tasks connected to development and continuous integration. This experience has shown me how important reliability, clear results, and teamwork are when software supports complex systems. It has also taught me to investigate issues methodically and make my work useful to teammates, not just get it working on my own machine.

Outside of my internship, I started Sunday Drive Games to keep developing games and improving my programming and creative skills. Building and releasing small games has required me to combine programming, design, debugging, art, and feedback. I enjoy the full process of creating a game, from the first idea to a playable result. My long-term goal is to continue growing as a software developer while building my independent game development work. I also value maintaining a healthy balance and making time for my family.

Professional Skills and Program Outcomes

Collaboration

My internship has shown me that software work is rarely done in isolation. Tests, scripts, and analysis are more useful when teammates can understand the results and apply them to their own work. My game projects have also taught me to seek feedback and consider how another person experiences what I have built. These experiences have helped me become more comfortable explaining my choices and revising my work.

Professional Communication

Through coursework, code reviews, documentation, and internship tasks, I have practiced explaining technical work for people who may not share the same context. The CS 499 narratives and this portfolio have pushed me to explain not only what I changed, but why I changed it and what I learned. I want my code, documentation, and explanations to be clear enough that another developer can follow my reasoning.

Algorithmic Problem-Solving

My CS-300 Advising Assistance Program demonstrates how data structures and algorithms affect a solution. I added binary search alongside hash-table searching, compared their behavior, and measured repeated search operations. This helped me consider trade-offs instead of assuming one approach is always best. It also reinforced that measured performance depends on the data and test conditions, not only on theoretical time complexity.

Computing Practices and Tools

My internship work with Python, automated testing, debugging, and log analysis has helped me apply software engineering practices to practical problems. In my CS-300 project, I improved input handling and error reporting and added course-data validation for duplicate course IDs and prerequisites that do not exist in the loaded data. My independent game projects provide another example of applying programming tools and iterative development to produce a usable result.

Security Mindset and Professional Integrity

My code review helped me look beyond whether a program functions and consider weaknesses in its design, input handling, and treatment of sensitive information. My internship has also reinforced that security and integrity matter in everyday scripting and testing, not just in large applications. A small script can still affect important data or influence decisions, so I need to be careful about what it changes, how it handles information, and whether its results are trustworthy.

I have also learned how important it is to speak up when something does not seem right instead of ignoring a concern just because it is uncomfortable or inconvenient. In a professional environment, overlooking a problem or misrepresenting a result can have serious consequences for the team, the company, and the people who depend on the work. I want to be someone who takes responsibility, documents concerns honestly, and asks questions when a result or practice seems wrong. I still have more to learn about security, but I understand that protecting systems and maintaining professional integrity are responsibilities throughout development.

How the Portfolio Fits Together

The artifacts in this portfolio use one project, the CS-300 Advising Assistance Program, to demonstrate different areas of my development. The software design and engineering enhancement focuses on input validation and error handling. The algorithms and data structures enhancement compares hash-table lookup with binary search and includes performance testing. The database-focused enhancement checks course data for duplicate identifiers and prerequisites that are not present in the loaded records. Keeping the same artifact allowed me to build on existing work and demonstrate several types of improvement without starting over each time.

The code review provides a starting point for understanding the project and identifying areas for improvement, while the three narratives explain the reasoning and learning behind each enhancement. Together, these pieces show my ability to review existing code, make targeted changes, test results, and reflect on trade-offs. I also draw on my internship and independent game development to show how I apply these skills in different settings. I see this portfolio as evidence of my progress and as a starting point for continued growth as a software engineer and game developer.
